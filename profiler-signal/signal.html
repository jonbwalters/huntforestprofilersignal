Definition · mathematics · scoring

What the signal is, and how it's calculated

The profiler signal is a daily statistical watch on four motion-controller telemetry channels. It asks one question every production day: are the profiler infeed rolls tracking their commanded position the way they normally do? When the answer is no for two production days in a row, the signal fires.

The four channels

The canter line's profilers are positioned by servo axes. For each axis the control system reports the difference between the commanded position and the actual position — the following error. A healthy axis holds that error small and steady. A degrading component — a worn bearing, a failing hydraulic cylinder, a stretching chain, a dying drive — shows up as a sustained change in that error, days before the component fails outright.

Channel selection is part of the signal. These four axes sit on the components that dominate the profiler's failure record — the infeed rolls and their bearings, cylinders, chains, and drives. Widening the group to other profiler channels (amperages, position axes) was tested and it dilutes the signal; the narrow group is what validated.

How it's calculated

  1. Keep producing time only. A 5-minute bucket counts when the line is actually cutting: the trimmer is receiving boards (trimmer_input_board_count > 50, trimmer_trimvol_total > 0) and the canter line is feeding (oli_cant_qty > 0). Idle time, jams, and 4×4-bypass runs never enter the statistics — zeros during idle are sensor behaviour, never signal.
  2. Summarise each production day. For each channel c and day d, take the median of its producing buckets.
    mc(d) = median( readings during producing buckets of day d )
    The median ignores single-bucket spikes; only a shift in the day's typical behaviour moves it.
  3. Build the channel's own baseline. Each day is compared against that channel's trailing 30 days, shifted one day so today never judges itself.
    Mc(d) = median of mc over the prior 30 days
    Sc(d) = robust standard deviation of the same window Robust SD = IQR ÷ 1.349 (the reference implementation uses the equivalent MAD × 1.4826). Both estimate one standard deviation while ignoring outliers, so one bad day never distorts the yardstick.
  4. Score the deviation.
    zc(d) = ( mc(d) − Mc(d) ) / Sc(d) How many normal days' worth of variation today sits away from this channel's own recent normal. |z| ≥ 3 is a deviation day — roughly a 1-in-300 event per channel-day under normal behaviour. Deviations count in both directions.
  5. Fire on persistence, never on a single day. The signal fires when any of the four channels holds |z| ≥ 3 for two consecutive production days. One wild day is noise; two in a row is a component that has changed state. This persistence rule is why the record contains no false alarms — and a third deviation day, or a re-fire inside an open warning, is the escalation tier (see the response protocol on the Overview).

The warning window, and how it clears

Repair-aware clearing — the operational rule

  • The warning stays open while any channel keeps deviating.
  • When the channels return within ±2 robust SD of baseline, a five-day timer runs; five consecutive calm days close the window.
  • A documented repair followed by two calm production days closes it immediately — the telemetry confirms the fix.
  • A calm return with no documented repair holds the warning on the five-day timer rather than clearing it. An unexplained recovery is not trusted. June 2026 vindicated this: the channels calmed without a repair, re-deviated, and the roll replacements landed in July.
  • A re-deviation inside an open window is a re-fire — the escalation signature, "the fix did not hold." December 30 and June 16–19 are the in-record examples.

Flat seven-day scoring — the audit rule

The stricter convention never looks at the telemetry after the firing. It asks only whether a documented profiler event followed within seven days. Fully mechanical, and hand-checkable.

Scoring: what counts as a hit

A firing scores a hit when a documented profiler event falls inside its window — a costed canter-line stop attributed to the profiler, a reactive work order on the profiler, or an operator-ledger repair record naming it. Everything is scored against the mill's own records; nothing is self-graded from the telemetry.

Verification behind the numbers

Specificity
The same firings were scored against every other machine centre's failures. They predict none of them — the signal is profiler-specific and does not fire on mill-wide chaos.
Re-derivation
A second analysis environment was given only the written rule — no scripts, no thresholds as implemented, no firing dates — and asked to reconstruct the signal from raw telemetry. It rediscovered essentially the same episodes, produced no false alarms across its implementation variants, and returned p = 0.060 against the original p = 0.054.
Prospective
The analysis was frozen on data ending March 2026. The signal then fired in the spring, recorded as an open prediction before any outcome data existed in the workspace. The July 2026 work-order extract confirmed the outcome: a reactive replacement on exactly the deviating side — WO188731, "REPLACE LEFT PROFILER" (Event 9).
Rule selection
The two-day rule was adopted after a head-to-head against the original three-day rule on identical machinery (2026-09-01 deep dive): the 2-day warning set contains every 3-day warning, fires earlier on all of them, covers 11 of 14 failure episodes against 8 of 14, and holds zero false alarms. It also times better against the weekly PM: eight of nine warnings went live with a Tuesday walk still ahead of the failure evidence, against three of six for the 3-day rule.

Limitations, stated plainly

  • The signal is one-directional. It covers 11 of the 14 profiler failure episodes on record. Of the three it misses, two are single-day sudden breaks — sudden failures do not drift first — and one begins after the telemetry record ends. Silence guarantees nothing. A firing, on this record, has always meant something is physically wrong.
  • Nine warnings is a small sample. The honest statistical label is permutation p ≈ 0.05–0.11: dominant over the 3-day rule on every measured axis, suggestive, and short of proof. Every additional prospectively-scored warning tightens it.
  • The warning state is open ~165 calendar days per year under this rule (95 under 3-day) — about 45% of the calendar. A standing warning treated as a siren will be tuned out; the two-tier protocol exists for exactly this reason. Tier 1 costs nothing (it aims a walk that already happens); only tier 2 escalates.
  • Outcome coverage thins after early March 2026. The costed-stop log and the operator ledger end there — the ledger moved to a new system — so the last two warnings are scored on work orders alone, and an unticketed crew fix of the kind documented in Event 1 would be invisible.
  • Threshold sensitivity is real. Warning counts move with the |z| threshold (12 / 9 / 7 / 6 at 2.5 / 3.0 / 3.5 / 4.0), so the rule should always be quoted with its implementation pinned. The 3-day era's July-versus-October derivation swap is resolved under this rule — both arcs fire as separate warnings in every derivation.
